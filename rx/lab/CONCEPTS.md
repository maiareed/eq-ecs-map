# Rx Lab: concepts

Three live models, one per question a woman over 40 should be able to answer after a few minutes:
what is a peptide, how does NAD+ work, and why does LDN's dose matter. Each opens in a working
state, teaches through what she does with her hands, and keeps copy to a caption beside the model.
They share one page (`index.html`, hash routes `#beads`, `#spark`, `#dial`) entered from the ECS
map's line-art figure. Simulations are illustrations, labeled as such; numbers come from
`rx/review/research/`.

## Bead by Bead (what a peptide is)
A chain-physics sandbox. She taps amino acids from a tray of all 20, colored by how they behave
around water, and each one snaps onto a wobbling chain. The model recognizes real molecules as she
builds them: KPV, GHK (a copper ion flies in and grips it, which is the "Cu" in GHK-Cu), oxytocin
(its two cysteines close a ring), BPC-157 and MOTS-c. "Let it fold" turns on a toy attraction
between water-shy beads so a long chain balls up the way proteins do. "Line them up" pours every
molecule into rows of 40 beads, the FDA's peptide/protein cutoff, so KPV is a speck, tirzepatide
nearly fills one row and albumin fills fifteen. Naltrexone and NAD+ appear as weight-equivalents to
show they aren't chains at all. Why it's first: "peptide" is the word she's heard and can't define,
and building one is the fastest way to own it.

## The Spark (how NAD+ works)
A cell in real time. NAD+ molecules are shuttles: empty gold rings pick up electrons from broken-down
fuel, turn into glowing NADH, ride to the mitochondrion and drop the electrons into its folded inner
membrane, which throws off an energy spark. Then they go back for more. Other enzymes (CD38 at the
cell surface, sirtuins and PARPs at the nucleus) break shuttles apart, and a recycler rebuilds them.
An age slider raises CD38's appetite, the direction mouse studies point to, and she watches the
shuttle pool thin and the spark meter drop. "Add NAD+ from outside" floods the cell and lets her see
the same enzymes chew it back down. Tap anything to learn what it is. The copy stays honest: precursors
raise blood NAD+ in small human trials, benefits are unsettled, and nasal NAD+ hasn't been studied in
people yet.

## The Dial (why LDN's dose matters)
A patch of nerve-cell surface lined with opioid receptors, with her own endorphins docking and
lighting them up. A dose dial (off, 1 mg, 4.5 mg, 50 mg) releases naltrexone molecules that fit the
receptors without switching them on. A 24-hour clock runs from a bedtime dose: at 4.5 mg the block is
heavy overnight and fades by morning, while at 50 mg it holds all day. A live chart under the scene
draws the block and, as a dotted line, the leading theory: a short block prompts the body to turn up
its own endorphins, so the signal comes back stronger. She can drag a single molecule onto a receptor
to feel the lock-and-key. The chart is conceptual, built on the label's 4-hour and 13-hour half-lives,
and says so.

## Why these three, and what's next
They map one-to-one to the three questions, and each needs a simulation to teach: chains, flows and
time curves are exactly what static pages explain badly. Next would be the body-to-molecule zoom that
places every product (tirzepatide at the gut and brain, GHK-Cu in skin, BPC-157 in tissue) in the
same figure, reusing these three models as its deepest zoom levels.
