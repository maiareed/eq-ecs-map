Human studies of LDN are small, and some early results didn't hold up in larger trials. A second idea, from lab and animal work: naltrexone may also quiet immune cells in the brain through a different receptor, TLR4. Because it blocks opioid receptors, naltrexone isn't combined with opioid pain medicine. A licensed clinician decides whether it fits you, and at what strength.

Sources
  1. Naltrexone label: blocks opioid receptors; 50 mg tablets; half-lives of 4 and 13 hours
  2. Naltrexone label: not used with opioid pain medicine
  3. LDN defined as 1 to 5 mg a day (Toljan and Vrooman 2018, review)
  4. 4.5 mg a day, taken at bedtime, in a 2013 crossover trial (Younger 2013)
  5. The rebound theory: a short block may raise endorphins and receptors (2016 review)
  6. The TLR4 immune-cell idea, from lab work (Younger 2014, review)
  7. Small trials and mixed results across 105 studies (Gouda 2026, review)

The block curve uses the label's 4-hour and 13-hour half-lives in a simple model. Its height and the endorphin line are conceptual, not pharmacokinetic data.
