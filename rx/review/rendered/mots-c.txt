MOTS-C RX · HOW IT WORKS
A message your mitochondria send out.

Most of what mitochondria do stays inside the cell. MOTS-c is different: a small peptide your mitochondria make that appears to travel and act elsewhere. Almost all of what we know comes from lab and animal work.

You've kept moving and you still notice it: workouts land differently than they did, and your body seems to respond to effort on a slower clock.

MOTS-c gets attention because exercise appears to raise it, and because in animals it acts on the same energy-sensing switch that exercise flips. Whether giving it to people does anything comparable hasn't been tested in a published trial.[1][2][3]

Made inside, sent outside

MOTS-c is coded in your mitochondria's own DNA. It leaves the mitochondrion and, in lab studies, switches on an energy sensor in the cell.

What we know, and what we don't

Most rungs here sit low on the evidence ladder.

It's coded in your mitochondria

Your mitochondria carry their own small set of DNA. MOTS-c is one of the few peptides made from it.[2]

Established
In animals, it acts on an energy switch

In mice, MOTS-c switched on AMPK and changed how the body used sugar and fat.[2]

Lab and animal research
Exercise appears to raise it

In one small study of young men, a single workout raised MOTS-c in muscle and, more modestly, in blood.[1]

Early human studies
Age findings are mixed

In men, blood MOTS-c was lower with age while muscle MOTS-c was higher. There isn't a clean story here yet.[4]

Early human studies
No published human trials of MOTS-c itself

A modified version was tested in a small early trial, with results shared only in a company press release.[3]

Made here

MOTS-c is coded by a short stretch of mitochondrial DNA inside a gene called 12S. It's 16 amino acids long.[2]

Established

Acts here

In cells and mice, MOTS-c switched on AMPK, an energy sensor, and cells used more sugar and fat. Under stress it moved into the nucleus.[2]
