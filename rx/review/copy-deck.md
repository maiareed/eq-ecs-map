What is a peptide, really?

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, the same building blocks that make up proteins. The difference is length. Here's how the molecules you'll see at Equilibria compare, bead for bead.

Peptides are suddenly everywhere: in skincare, on podcasts, in the group chat. The same word gets used for a face cream and an injection, which makes it hard to know what you're actually looking at.

Size is the simplest way in. Each bead below is one amino acid. Count the beads and you know what kind of molecule you're dealing with. You'll also see why the word peptide on its own tells you so little.

## LDN explainer (rx/ldn.html)

LDN RX · HOW IT WORKS
Same molecule. A very different idea.

Standard naltrexone is a 50 mg daily tablet. Low-dose naltrexone, as studied, uses a small fraction of that. Here's why researchers think the size of the dose may change what it does, and what's still an open question.

Maybe a friend mentioned LDN, or you saw it in a forum thread. Then you looked it up and found a medicine best known at a much higher dose, for a very different purpose. Both of those things are true.

LDN is naltrexone at doses studied around 1 to 5 mg a day, compared with the 50 mg tablet the FDA approved. Your clinician sets your strength. Researchers think a dose this small may act differently: a short, light touch on the same receptors. The leading theory is that your body responds to the touch.[1][2]

Two theories, both still being tested

Nobody has fully pinned down how LDN works. These are the two explanations researchers take most seriously, followed by an honest read of the human data.

The rebound idea

A brief block on opioid receptors may prompt your body to make more of its own endorphins and add more receptors. Over a full day, the net effect could be more of your own signaling, not less. Most of the support for this comes from animal and cell research.[3]

Leading theory
The immune-cell idea

Separately from opioid receptors, naltrexone appears to quiet a receptor called TLR4 on microglia, the resident immune cells of the brain and spinal cord. In lab work, a mirror-image form of naltrexone that doesn't touch opioid receptors still blocked TLR4. Researchers note the data don't fit this explanation perfectly.[4]

Lab and animal research
What the human studies show so far

Most human studies of LDN have been small, with few repeats. A 2026 review of 105 studies, 15 of them randomized trials, found it was generally well tolerated but that the evidence lacks methodological strength. Early positive results from uncontrolled studies were rarely repeated in placebo-controlled trials. That's the honest state of the science, and it's why a clinician decides whether it's worth trying for you.[4][5]

## NAD+ explainer (rx/nad.html)

ENERGY SPRAY RX · HOW IT WORKS
The molecule that keeps your cells' power line moving.

NAD+ isn't a peptide and it isn't a stimulant. It's a working part inside every cell, and research suggests you have less of it as you age. Here's what it does, what the evidence says, and where the real questions are.

The second coffee used to be optional. A hard week used to take a weekend to shake off, not the whole next week. When energy shifts like that, it's natural to wonder what's changing underneath.

NAD+ is a molecule your cells use to turn food into usable energy. Researchers are interested in it for two reasons: levels appear to fall with age, and it's now possible to raise them. Whether raising them changes how you feel day to day is still the open question.[1][2][3]

Inside the power plant

Mitochondria turn the food you eat into energy your cells can spend. NAD+ is the shuttle that keeps that line moving.

What we know, and what we don't

Four ideas, each with a different level of certainty.

It's a shuttle, not a stimulant

NAD+ carries electrons from the food you break down to the machinery that makes cellular energy. It's part of the basic equipment in every cell, the way a delivery truck is part of a supply chain.[1]

Established
Levels appear to drop with age

In skin samples from 49 people, newborns and adults aged 15 to 77, NAD+ was lower in older skin, in women and men. Only a handful of human studies have measured this directly.[2][3]

Early human studies
One reason it might drop

In mice, an enzyme called CD38 that uses up NAD+ rises with age and accounts for much of the decline. Whether the same holds in people is still being worked out.[4]

Lab and animal research
Raising it is possible. Feeling different is the open question.

Small human trials show that oral NAD+ building blocks can raise NAD+ in the blood. A 2025 review found that measurable benefits in people have been limited so far.[5][6][3]

## MOTS-c template test (rx/mots-c.html), internal only

Template test. Internal only.
MOTS-C RX · HOW IT WORKS
A message mitochondria send out.

Most of what mitochondria do stays inside the cell. MOTS-c is different: a small peptide made in mitochondria that appears to travel and act elsewhere. Almost all of what we know comes from lab and animal work.

You've kept moving and you still notice it: workouts land differently than they did, and your body seems to respond to effort on a slower clock.

MOTS-c gets attention because exercise appears to raise it, and because in animals it acts on the same energy-sensing switch that exercise flips. Whether giving it to people does anything comparable hasn't been tested in a published trial.[1][2][3]

Made inside, sent outside

MOTS-c is coded in your mitochondria's own DNA. It leaves the mitochondrion and, in lab studies, switches on an energy sensor in the cell.
